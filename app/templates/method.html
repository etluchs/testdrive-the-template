How the numbers are made

The question

For a hero, an item, and an enemy team: at which point in the match does buying that item help most, and when does it stop being worth it?

Why the obvious answer is wrong

Group purchases by minute, average the win column, and you get a curve that rises steadily. It is not measuring the item. A player who buys a tier-4 item at minute 15 could afford it at minute 15, which usually means the game was already going well. The purchase is a symptom of the lead, not the cause of it.

Billy’s Phantom Strike is a clean example. Taken raw, its win rate climbs from about 46% for early buys to about 63% around minute 30 — which would read as “buy it as late as possible”. Two things produce most of that climb: players who reach minute 30 with the souls for it are winning, and losing games end before minute 30 at all, so late buys are drawn from a survivor-biased pool of long, close matches.

What this app does instead

Every purchase is compared against a reference population: players on the same hero, at the same minute, holding a similar net worth, whatever they bought. The reported effect is the difference between those two win rates, averaged across net-worth bands and weighted by where the buyers actually sit:

effect(m) = Σb ∈ B(m) wb · ( winratebuyers(m, b) − winratereference(m, b) ),   wb = purchases(m, b) / Σb ∈ B(m) purchases(m, b)

B(m) is the set of net-worth bands with at least 30 purchases and 30 reference players in that minute; thinner cells are dropped rather than estimated from, and the weights are normalised over what remains. The purchase counts shown in the tables are the same restricted total, so the sample size quoted is the one the number was actually computed from.

Net worth is banded in 5,000-soul steps and minutes in two-minute buckets. Confidence intervals are 95% normal-approximation intervals on the difference of two proportions. Where a single best window is picked out of the 21 on offer — ranking items for a build — the bound is widened by a Bonferroni correction across those 21 comparisons, because the winning window is otherwise partly just the luckiest one.

Why not every team composition

Deadlock never repeats a hero in a match, so with 39 playable heroes there are C(39,6) × C(33,6) / 2 ≈ 1.8 trillion possible 6-v-6 matchups. The warehouse holds roughly 10.7 million matches over the last 180 days. Enumerating matchups is therefore impossible by about seven orders of magnitude, and no amount of further collection changes that: at ~57,000 matches a day, covering them once would take millions of years.

Restricting to the 10,000 most-played compositions does not rescue it either. Measured over the same window:

Split 1,131 games across 251 items and 21 purchase windows and there is nothing left to measure. So the enemy team is handled by the presence of individual enemy heroes rather than by exact composition. That is not only what the data can support — it is closer to the actual mechanism. Phantom Strike is good into Vindicta because she is squishy, mobile and hard to reach, not because of her identity. Effects estimated per enemy hero generalise to compositions that have never been played; a lookup table of exact compositions could not.

Each result states which level produced it, and how many purchases sit underneath. Where the enemy team is too thin to adjust for, the app says so and falls back to the hero-and-item estimate rather than inventing a number.

Only the current patch

The timing numbers come from matches played since the most recent major patch, which the API identifies separately from the frequent minor updates that tweak one hero. Statistics from before it describe a different game.

The item list is filtered the same way. Of the 251 upgrades in the catalogue, 78 have been removed from the game and survive only so that old matches can still be decoded — several never had a display name at all. A further 17 exist only in Street Brawl, which is a different mode from the one these statistics describe. That leaves the 156 items currently buyable in the standard shop, across tiers 1 to 4.

Components are included. Healing Booster builds into Healing Tempo, but it is an ordinary tier-2 item that players buy and hold on its own, and often the real decision early in a match is between components rather than between the tier-4 items they eventually become. So each is estimated in its own right, and the picker groups items by tier so the cheap ones are not buried.

Comparing patches

Deadlock does not publish version numbers — updates are identified by date and headline, and the only numeric identifiers are opaque client build ids. So the patch picker names each era by the day it started and the title of the update that started it.

{{ alert( "A patch figure is not the same number as the timing curve. The curve compares an item against all players on one hero; a patch figure compares it against all other purchases at the same net worth in the same era, pooled across every hero. An item's own purchases are excluded from its own reference, which means that when the rest of the shop shifts — a different item is buffed, or players change what they buy — an item whose own win rate never moved can still show a change.", kind="warning", title="What a patch comparison measures") }}

When two patches are compared directly, both are weighted by the combined net-worth mix of their buyers rather than each by its own. Otherwise a change in who buys an item — richer players picking it up after a buff elsewhere — shows up as the item itself changing. On the test case built for this, that artifact was worth 7.6 percentage points on an item whose win rate had not moved at all.

Data

Aggregates are precomputed offline from the community-run Deadlock API at api.deadlock-api.com, which is not operated by Valve. It is named here rather than linked: this app loads nothing from another host, and an outbound link would hand that host a referrer for every reader who followed it. Its /v1/sql endpoint allows 20 requests per hour, so the app never queries it live — it reads a precomputed extract. Players who opt out of data collection are absent from the warehouse, which is a source of non-random missingness this app cannot correct for.
